Executive Summary

3 November 2026 · Valuation date 30 September 2026

In August we put fair value at $285.83 a share and said, in effect, that the market had handed the opportunity back to buyers. Then SpaceX reported its second public quarter, and the AI business did something AI businesses rarely do: it made buckets of money. Super Intelligence revenue nearly tripled quarter on quarter to $7.56B at a mind-bending 65.5% adjusted EBITDA margin, total revenue reached $14.575B (+161% year on year) and SpaceX guided Q1 2027 revenue to $33-37B (Q3 8-K; Q3 call). Maybe they should in fact change the ticker to SXSI because this is absolutely the sexiest asset on the planet, or off. At the current valuation against guided YE27 ARR, you owe it to your bloodline to own some SPCX.

We rebuilt the model from the ground up around physical units: gigawatts of compute energised, rent and build cost per gigawatt, Starship phasing out Falcon 9 flights by 2029, Starlink satellites in orbit, delivering increased subscribers. Every flight now has a job, every gigawatt has a buyer, and the total is capped by what the world actually spends on Superintelligence infrastructure. The result is a base-case fair value of $352.75 a share (bear $211.42, bull $513.73), on a highly conservative 10% discount rate (up from 9%), 4% terminal growth, 13,811M diluted shares.

SPCX closed at $136.37 on 2 November (Yahoo Finance), roughly 2.6x below our base and still below the bear. This market is, in effect, paying for Starlink and the current Falcon 9 launch business and getting the multi-gigawatt compute empire consuming GPUs and power like a sandworm in Dune. We would like to thank Wall St for its lack of willingness to thoughtfully price a conglomerate in the 21st century and the resultant generosity. We continue to accumulate and yield with Starloop from Dialectic, now producing consistently double-digit yields on ySPCXc.

The honest caveat sits in one number: rent. Our base assumes customers pay $60B per gigawatt-year by the end of 2027, rising to about $80B by 2030 before fading 2% a year from 2032. That is above Goldman's spot rate card ($40-50B) and well above term pricing ($20-30B). If rent stays flat at $60B, fair value falls to $311.86. If the SI boom politely declines to keep booming (which we see absolutely no evidence of whatsoever), see the bear case. Either way, the upside is literally out of this world; the dispersion rewards those willing to cut through the noise and act with conviction.

What we learned this quarter

  • SpaceX is an SI compute business in the short term as this is the most profitable business on the planet. Super Intelligence revenue ($7.56B) exceeded Space plus Connectivity combined ($7.02B). Annualised, SI earned about $19.5B per average gigawatt (Dialectic arithmetic), comfortably above the $9-14B we used in August. Revenue per GW is the single number that moved this model most.
  • The margins are real. SI at 65.5% and Connectivity at 66.7% adjusted EBITDA. Payback on compute build is 7-11 months depending on the configuration and the contract, but payback continues to trend down. This reminds us of the early days of Bitcoin mining.
  • Starlink missed slightly on subscribers and beat on price. 13.6M consumer subscribers versus 14.29M expected, but ARPU of $69 beat. We model 15.2M by year end and keep $69 flat; the dynamic-pricing pilot in Kansas and Missouri (list price x0.8 to x1.2) suggests SpaceX would rather price for capacity than give it away (PCMag).
  • Earnings quality. Other income and a tax benefit added $1.415B, about 32% of $4.404B net income (Dialectic arithmetic on the 8-K). Operating income of $2.79B and the EBITDA beat (+33% vs consensus) are the cleaner reads.
  • Launch counts fell while tonnage per launch rose. Starship will replace Falcon 9 faster than we had previously projected. 35 launches (12 customer, 23 internal) versus 44 a year ago, as Falcon 9 makes room for Starship. Flight 14 on 28 September was the first revenue Starship mission, deploying 26 Starlink V3 satellites. Starship Flight 15 is now NET 12 November and will attempt the first ship catch (NASASpaceflight). One website reported it had already flown, which it hadn't; we model rockets, not wishes.

What Changed Since $286

Our August target price of $285.83 came from our v5 model, which valued each business on revenue and margin paths. v10 is a different machine: each business starts from its physical drivers and the segments add up, to the cent, to a consolidated DCF. That makes a like-for-like bridge from $285.83 impossible, so we show two things instead: how the inputs moved, and the documented step changes inside the new build.

A note on discipline. Early drafts of the physical model let every gigawatt rent at full market price forever, and counted xAI's own use of its own data centres as rent it paid itself. Two fixes tamed it: xAI's own capacity is now valued at what Grok, Cursor and X actually earn, and total compute is capped at SpaceX's share of global SI infrastructure spend. Much cleaner and still super SXSI (read, ‘sexy’).

The Physical Inputs

Spreadsheets are cheap; gigawatts, rockets and satellites are not. Every revenue line starts with something you could, in principle, photograph from a helicopter or satellite right now. Here are the six charts that drive the model, all in the base case with bear and bull shown where they differ.

1. Compute online: from 1.7 GW to 30 GW

Terrestrial compute goes from 1.7 GW at 30 September to 2.75 GW at year end and 13.0 GW by the end of 2027, the level management said it would be "surprised" to miss. After that, the demand cap takes the wheel: 13.25 GW in 2028, 20.2 GW in 2030 and 24.1 GW in 2035, with orbital data centres taking the total to 30.3 GW. The bear reaches 10 GW by end-2027 (the JPMorgan, Deutsche Bank and Raymond James cluster); the bull reaches 16 GW. This is absolutely enabled by SpaceX’s ability to produce gas turbines. Right now they are focused on vanes and blades; we expect them to produce gas turbines end-to-end by the end of the decade while adding an unimaginable amount of solar to the Texas landscape and then to the stars.

The cap is SpaceX's share of global SI infrastructure spend, one path for every case: about $1.0T in 2026, $3.0T in 2030 (the low end of Nvidia's $3-4T outlook), then +6.2% a year to $4.05T in 2035 (Dell'Oro and Morgan Stanley data-centre capex paths; ABI's 8.0% brackets it). Global SI spend 20% lower from 2029 costs $58.06 a share; 20% higher adds the same.

One quirk worth admitting: the model builds 10 GW in 2027 and then barely adds in 2028 as the cap binds, so 2027 capex is $425B and 2028 capex only $35B. Real life will be smoother. Value is not very sensitive to the timing, but the funding need is (see Financing).

2. Rent and build cost per gigawatt

External rent ramps from $42B per GW-year in Q1 2027 to $60B by Q4 2027 (2027 average $51B), then grows 10% a year to 2030 (bear 5%, bull 20%), is flat in 2031 and fades 2% a year from 2032 (bear 3%, bull 1%). Base rent ends 2035 at $73.7B. Orbital capacity rents at the same price as terrestrial; a 20% orbital premium would add only $6.36 a share, so we don't count on it.

Build cost per new gigawatt is $40B in 2027 (Vera Rubin era, near Milk Road's ~$45B), $36B in 2028, then a $32B floor to 2031 (JPMorgan cites management "mid-$30Bs", Deutsche Bank $35-36B, ARK $32.6B for the latest build) before falling 2% a year to $29.5B in 2035. SpaceX's own Q3 filing implies at least $36.4B per GW added over nine months (Dialectic arithmetic), and Musk says capex efficiency is "severely underestimated" (Teslarati). As analysts, it is our job to remember that every entrepreneur must necessarily be optimistic about their prospects in order to face the challenges of each day and continue to pull rabbits from hats. We adjust and discount accordingly. Every $10B per GW of extra build cost is worth -$39.31 a share.

Half of SpaceX's compute serves SpaceXSI itself. That half is valued at what Grok, Cursor and X earn per gigawatt (about $9B per GW-year in Q3), not at market rent, so blended revenue per GW is $44.4B in 2030 rather than $80B. We think this could change because Grok Bot is probably the best product we’ve used, maybe ever. Entire companies, including Dialectic, are beginning to run on a substrate of Grok Bots. The product is a revelation. This is where the competitive dynamic really gets interesting. As Grok Bots hit the enterprise flywheel, competitors who rent their compute from SpaceXSI today face a dual threat – losing on lack of compute supply and product features. As Elon alludes, ‘live by the sword, die by the sword’ – this is true in the Sport of Kings (foundation models).

3. Launches: Falcon 9 headed for early retirement, Starship gets the starting job next season

We have Starship flying 78 times in 2027 and 250 in 2028; Falcon 9 drops to 60 flights in 2027 and 24 in 2028 and is gone by 2029 (one Starship carries the Starlink capacity of about ten Falcon 9s). From there the base rises every year without much of a dip: 277 flights in 2029, 323 in 2030 and 636 in 2035. Each flight has a primary customer: Starlink growth and five-year replacement (204 a year), commercial and government satellites and lunar cargo (225 external missions by 2035), NASA HLS (6 a year), Mars windows (65 in 2035, cost only, no revenue), and then the granddaddy opportunity of them all – orbital data centres (96 in 2035), Starfall and a 5% test and spare allowance for other opportunities and maybe some Space Art.

For scale: SpaceX told the FAA it wants to launch 10,000 times a year, and Elon added that this is "just from Starbase Louisiana" (X, 28 Oct). Our 636 makes us, by a factor of about 15, the conservative ones in this conversation. We cut 276 flights from an earlier 912-flight 2035 path because we could not name a paying use for them. If Elon brings thousands of flights home within the decade, it is almost certainly for ODCs and the valuation numbers begin to extrapolate to a level that butt-hurt bears cannot comprehend and thusly we will avoid that debate for the time being and hold this celestial upside in the back pocket for stargazing nights.

4. Starlink: satellites, subscribers, price

Consumer subscribers grow from 15.2M at the end of 2026 to 23.2M in 2027, 56.2M in 2030 and 110.2M in 2035 (bear 68.2M, bull 158.4M), keeping steady at ARPU $69 a month, the Q3 actual, as that is a level that should wash the big three telecoms out over time. Direct-to-cell starts in 2028 at 15M subscribers and reaches 142M in 2035. The fleet grows from about 13,500 satellites at the end of 2026 to 35,700 in 2030 and 52,800 in 2035, with a fifth of it replaced each year. Connectivity revenue reaches $86.7B in 2030 and $183.0B in 2035, which is actually extremely conservative when you consider executives’ argument that connected devices will explode upward as robots come online en masse.

Not modelled, on purpose: a Boost Mobile purchase (talks only), the Grain 800 MHz spectrum price (undisclosed) and anything that requires US carriers to be friendly. T-Mobile's CEO says there is "no room" for a SpaceX MVNO. They can pound sand and will get consumed by the sandworm in the end.

5. Orbital data centres from 2030

Musk says orbital compute added per month "probably exceeds terrestrial around the end of 2028" and that future superintelligence compute "will round up to 100%" in space. We start later and dramatically smaller, as otherwise the valuation numbers become stratospheric: orbital data centres come online in 2030 at 0.6 GW and reach 6.2 GW by 2035 (about 20% of total compute), at the same rent as terrestrial (that may not be the correct way of looking at it), inside the same demand cap. They add $254B of EV in the base ($18.40 a share). Switching them off entirely moves fair value by only +$2.40 a share, because the cap reallocates that demand to the ground; the option is in timing and cost, not in extra demand.

Starfall: a small capsule with a big idea

SpaceX has put up a product page for Starfall, a return capsule that carries up to 1,000 kg for weeks-long microgravity research and manufacturing, with 1 kW of power, Ethernet and a Starlink link (spacex.com/starfall). There is no price. The Q3 release says three commercial missions are signed; Reuters names the first customer, Space Cargo Unlimited, flying on the first commercial Starship mission in 2028 (Reuters, 15 Sep).

We model it modestly: 4 capsules in 2027, 12 in 2028, 45 in 2030 and 200 in 2035, at $15M falling to $10M each (Varda, the nearest comparable, is around $2.5-12M per mission), at the Q3 Space margin of 20.2%. That is $0.06B of revenue in 2027, $0.59B in 2030 and $2.0B in 2035, worth $0.13 a share (bull $4.5B in 2035). Volumes and prices are Dialectic assumptions [FLAG] . Doubling the volumes adds $0.14. Starfall is a call option on in-space manufacturing and point-to-point cargo; we have priced the option, not the dream.

Financing: the $425B year

Building 10 GW in a year is expensive. Base capex is $425B in 2027, unlevered free cash flow is -$266B, and the cumulative trough is -$295B. After the $51B of net cash, the funding need is $244B, of which the ~$40B Apollo-managed GPU finance lease covers the first $40B (CNBC; 8-K 26 Oct). Peak new debt is $254B. In the P&L, existing debt costs 5.86% (the June investment-grade notes), new debt 8.0% (the October GPU financing priced ~7.75-8.3%) and cash earns 4.0% [FLAG] . Fitch affirmed BBB+ after the lease (Fitch).

Once new debt is repaid (over three years), the model pays out half of cash above a $20B minimum: $303B in 2030, $324B in 2032 and $514B in 2035 in the base. This is a policy assumption [FLAG] , not a forecast of what SpaceX will announce; it exists so that the cash pile doesn't earn absurd interest. The valuation is an unlevered DCF at 10%, so neither the interest nor the payout changes the fair value.

Street View: How We Compare

We are above most of the Street on price and roughly in line on 2027 physical capacity. The difference is mostly rent per gigawatt and how long it lasts. The smartest published 2028 numbers come from Sequoia's Shaun Maguire, a SpaceX shareholder since about the same time as us: $731B of revenue and 21 GW of terrestrial compute by the end of 2028, against our $585B and 13.25 GW. Run through our framework, his inputs give $300.56 a share, below our base, because his rent per gigawatt is lower over time. The Street is mostly arguing about the same multiplication while no one really allows themselves to price in all of the upside of space – ODCs, off-planet fabrication, lunar/Mars colonisation. Call it the X trolls that hold everyone back from recognising the real opportunities here.

News Since the Print, and What Could Go Wrong

The news (none of it changes the base case yet)

  • Anthropic is the big tenant. Anthropic's public S-1 shows SpaceX compute commitments of up to $120.5B through 2029, rising to about $3.1B a month once all contracted capacity is delivered (expected December). Initial periods end in January and March 2027; after that, either side can exit on 90 days' notice (Yahoo Finance). Bloomberg identifies Anthropic as "Customer B", 34.5% of Q3 revenue (Bloomberg Law). A further tenant pays $1.11B a month from 1 December, per the CFO (name undisclosed).
  • Boost Mobile talks. The Washington Post reports SpaceX is in talks with EchoStar to buy Boost Mobile (7.38M subscribers at end-Q2) or use its network, after the big three carriers declined. No price (Reuters via Investing.com). SPCX touched $142.05 intraday on the report and closed at $136.37. [NOT MODELLED]
  • Tesla merger. Piper Sandler calls an all-stock SpaceX-Tesla deal "more likely than not" and says many believable scenarios imply an initial re-pricing of less than ±15% (Investing.com). Both boards have special committees; there are no terms. Our Post-Q2 edition covers the governance maths. [NOT MODELLED]
  • Starship Flight 15 is NET 12 November, pending FAA approval of the booster and ship catches; Flight 16, likely the first from LC-39A, is aimed for December. Falcon 9 is paused in Florida while 39A is readied for Starship.
  • SpaceX is leaving the old launch business on purpose. Crew Dragon stops by 2030, no commercial Falcon 9 sales for missions after 2028, and it "will no longer sell rides to the Moon" (Ars Technica). Our Falcon 9 exit by 2029 matches.
  • Grok Bot ate the Halloween candy. Musk says serving costs were "unsustainably high relative to revenue" after paying users hit weekly limits days early, and limits were tightened (TechCrunch). Demand is not the problem; unit economics on the consumer side are a watch item.
  • Generosity, Musk-style. Musk has pledged one of his own SpaceX shares to every US child in 2027, about 72M shares (CNBC). These are existing shares, so there is no dilution, only a generation of very motivated Starship fans.

Risks

  • Rent normalisation. Our base rent ($60B per GW-year at end-2027, ~$80B in 2030) is above Goldman's spot card ($40-50B) and SemiAnalysis's $30-50B, and far above term pricing ($20-30B). This is the single largest judgement in the model. Flat $60B costs $40.89 a share; the bear's +5% path costs $23.53.
  • Political witch hunt. While this would be a tragic crime against humanity’s future, we must recognise this risk. Some Democratic commentators have called for investigations of the company and its directors. It is really difficult to imagine what plan B is to America as SpaceX’s/Elon’s home, but we would do everything to make Switzerland a neutral welcoming option if Elon wants to spend time here.
  • Customer concentration. One customer was 34.5% of Q3 revenue and can leave on 90 days' notice after early 2027. Anthropic itself reported Q3 revenue of $16.8B and positive cash flow (Axios), which helps, but concentration is concentration.
  • Funding and execution of the 2027 build. $425B of capex in one year, a $244B funding need and new debt at ~8%. Equity-funding the gap at market would cut base value to $327.52. Missing 13 GW by end-2027 and landing at 10 GW costs $71.81.
  • Starship cadence. Five Starship flights in 2026, 78 in 2027, 250 in 2028. Each step needs permits (the FAA is scoping up to 456 launches a year at Starbase) and catches that have not yet happened.
  • Legal and local. A federal judge let the NAACP's Clean Air Act suit over Colossus 2's gas turbines proceed (CNBC); Louisiana residents are suing over the Starbase tax deal. We expect further litigation and permitting challenges; adverse rulings could delay projects or add cost.
  • Sovereign competition. We were in India earlier this week and there is zero chance they ever let Starlink into the country. The Ambanis dominate it. Same story for China, potentially Canada (which is laughable) and much of western Europe. A range of countries won't rely on Starlink for government communications. None of this changes 2027, but it caps the long tail of government revenue outside the US.
  • Earnings quality and dilution. 32% of Q3 net income came from other income and a tax benefit; Cursor equity awards and lock-up expiries add supply. Shares at 14,143M instead of 13,811M cost $8.28.
Page 10

Conclusion

SpaceX's second public quarter turned our biggest assumption, that SI compute would be a real business at GOAT margins, into reality. In order to keep the numbers where the compute is for the time being – on Earth – we raised the discount rate, capped demand at what the world can spend, gave every rocket a job and every gigawatt a tenant, and still arrived at $352.75 a share in the base case, 23% above August and about 2.6x the market price.

The bear ($211.42) is above today's price. The bull ($513.73) needs nothing more heroic than mid-teens gigawatts by end-2027 and rent that grows 20% a year to 2030. The risks are concentrated in political sea changes, customer concentration on compute and launch and increasing financing costs. We continue to accumulate SPCX at current levels and put it to yield in Starloop. The 5-year $10T aspiration from both previous reports stands tall. We will update the model when Flight 15 flies and when the Q4 print tells us how many gigawatts actually switched on. Ad astra.
